persistence mesh, the mutation-research plane, the codegen room where an

THE THROUGH-LINES, AT EXPERT GRAIN (both inherited from advanced)

  TWO BITS, NEVER ONE
      11  eligible to be targeted / actually attached
      14  a composition EXISTS / it is the CURRENT one for its lane
      15  a stamp is SET / a record inherited it
  Presence never implies liveness, and reach never implies grant.

  MELDER NEVER SUBSTITUTES
      10  a typo'd override path REFUSES; it does not hit the nearest
          socket, and at depth four that would be undetectable
      12  a rejected validation returns a VERDICT; it does not run a
          safer version of your code
      14  a rediscovery REFUSES; you cannot end up with two names for
          one subsystem
      14  a foresight read with no live custody REFUSES; it does not
          return an empty report that reads like "nothing found"
      15  an empty campaign REFUSES ON THE WRITE because the READ cannot
          address it
  The 15 case is the sharpest statement of it in the library: melder
  would rather fail your write than let you create a fact that nobody
  can ever ask a question about.

  THIRD THROUGH-LINE, VISIBLE ONLY AT THIS TIER - ABSENCE IS TYPED
  Advanced 15 taught it on the read surface (hidden vs empty, and the
  probe that tells them apart). Expert repeats it at three more grains,
  and the shape is identical every time: melder gives the two kinds of
  nothing two different spellings.
      11  "not allowed by policy" vs "rift_enabled is False" - refused
          by the OBSERVER, or refused by the WORLD
      12  ValueError on a malformed REQUEST vs a verdict object on
          disallowed CODE
      14  a refusal when custody is off vs unknown_custody_members when
          custody is on and the member simply has none
      16  "requires dynamic mode" vs "disabled for the current frame
          posture" - what a world IS vs what it is ALLOWED to do
  If you only ever learn one thing from this tier, learn to ask which
  KIND of nothing you are holding.

  FOURTH THROUGH-LINE, AND 16 IS WHERE IT BECOMES OBVIOUS -
  DECLARE INTENT, NOT POSSESSION
      14  a composition names a SET so you can ask about the set
      15  a campaign names an EFFORT so work can be attributed to it
      16  a claim mode names WHAT YOU INTEND TO DO to a scope, which is

  ADDENDUM AFTER THE FIRST RUN - AND IT IS THE MORE USEFUL HALF.
  Reading the source of a CALL tells you what the call does. It does not
  tell you WHAT THE DEFAULTS AROUND IT ARE, and both first-run failures
  were exactly that gap:
      - `_validate_target_frame_names` reads perfectly innocent. The bug
        was 200 lines away in a defaults dict: allowed_target_frame_names
        ships as ("default",), not as ().
      - `research_group_footprint` reads as a plain delegation. The bug
        was one frame deeper, in `_require_live_custody`.
  SO: when a lesson SETS UP a subsystem, read its defaults table, not
  just the verbs it calls. A default is a decision someone made and it is
  as much part of the contract as a signature.

FINDINGS FROM THE FIRST RUN (2026-08-03) - 4 of 6 new lessons were RED
  Two causes, four files, and both causes became lesson content because
  the real behaviour was more interesting than the assumed behaviour.

  CAUSE 1 (11, 12, 13) "Target frame 'X' is not allowed by Nexus policy."
    Every lesson postured its frames correctly and never told the NEXUS
    it was allowed to look at them. The draft of lesson 11 claimed THREE
    gates, all of them posture. There are two gate GROUPS owned by two
    PARTIES, and the policy group runs first. The corrected lesson is
    strictly better: it now proves BOTH refusal directions using two
    frames with identical posture, which is the only way to show that
    neither party can grant alone.

  CAUSE 2 (14) "Crystallizer custody is unavailable (not recording)."
    The draft treated the six composition reads as one uniform family and
    asserted, in a printed line, that custody-less members would be
    reported honestly rather than raising. HALF RIGHT, AND THE HALVES
    MATTER: with custody OFF the read refuses; with custody ON, a member
    that has no crystal IS reported honestly under
    unknown_custody_members. Conflating those is conflating "I cannot
    answer" with "the answer is none", which is the exact error melder's
    design goes out of its way not to make. The lesson now demonstrates
    the refusal, activates the crystallizer, and then shows the same
    three reads answering - with the honest-absence data at the end.

  NOT A CAUSE, WORTH RECORDING: lessons 10 and 15 passed first time. Both
  are lessons where every claim came from a docstring CONTRACT block
  rather than from reading a call chain. That is a small sample, but it
  points the same way as the addendum above.

    `Rift.list_accessible_non_nexus_frame_names()` and the Nexus-level
    `list_accessible_non_nexus_frame_names(rift_id)` are marked
    `Internal` in their own docstrings. They are the best AIX door in the
    subsystem - they run BOTH attachment gates filtered by the rift's
    space type, so an agent can survey its reach instead of discovering
    a boundary by crossing it. An agent that cannot enumerate must probe,
    and probing an authority boundary is precisely what the rest of this
    design is built to avoid. Lesson 13 uses it and says plainly that it
    is Internal-marked rather than teaching it as public surface.
  - THE DEFAULT ALLOW-LIST IS A ONE-NAME TUPLE, NOT EMPTY.
    `allowed_target_frame_names` ships as ("default",). Because the check
    is skipped when the tuple is falsy, an EMPTY list means "unrestricted"
    - so the shipped value is the tight end of the dial, not the loose
    one. Worth a doc line somewhere prominent: the first thing anyone
    does with a rift is point it at a frame they named themselves, and
    the refusal message ("not allowed by Nexus policy") does not hint
    that a default allow-list is what they collided with.
  - `Conduit.transaction` ABORTS ON `Exception`, NOT ON `BaseException`
    (2026-08-03, lesson 16). The context manager is try/except/else, so
    a KeyboardInterrupt, SystemExit, or a GeneratorExit from the
    surrounding frame leaves the window OPEN with its claims held -
    which under a bounded-wait plane means other requests wait out the
    full `max_transaction_wait_time_in_seconds` for a session nobody is
    driving. Narrow, but the plane's own cleanup notes say a resisting
    session must not prevent teardown, so the intent is clearly that
    windows close. Worth a `finally` or an explicit BaseException arm.
    NOT pinned by a probe yet - raising BaseException inside an example
    would take the example runner with it.
  - SPELL IDS ARE PROCESS-WIDE AND THE FRAME IS NOT IN THE FINGERPRINT.
    Identical bindings in two frames COLLIDE. Every multi-frame lesson
    here passes binding_name= or spellframe= for that reason. It is the
    single most common way a working example goes red when copied.

THE TIER'S OWN SHAPE, NOW THAT IT IS DONE
  It opens with a COLD BOOT (01) and closes with a document that answers
  BEFORE any boot at all (18). In between it goes: record what changed
  (03-06), let an agent write into a running world (07-15), let several
  agents mutate it at once (16), and prepare a swap without taking it
  (17). Read in order, the tier is a widening circle of who is allowed
  to change what - and every widening is paid for with a new way to
  REFUSE precisely. That is the through-line worth naming out loud:
  melder buys concurrency and agency with precision of refusal, never
  with permissiveness.
